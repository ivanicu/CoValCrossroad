R817 · THE NORMALISATION CURVE — does the arc's ordering survive removing the tie handicap?
written before the run · NBOOT 1200 · ZEFF 2.801585

PROVOCATION
R816 established that the `world`→`personal` shift is driven by the TIE RATE: a strict-signed arm
cannot match a tied human pair, so its attainable A2 is bounded by 1 − tie_rate. Its NEXT proposed
re-expressing each committed A2 as a fraction of its own prompt's attainable maximum.

⛔ CHECK #419 FOUND THAT THIS ARC HAS ALREADY BEEN BURNED BY EXACTLY THAT OPERATION, TWICE.
 (1) R793 swept three normalisations of a ceiling -- raw, ÷CEIL_H, ÷√CEIL_H -- and got
     **WORLD A / WORLD B / WORLD A**. The verdict flipped with the choice, and R793 flagged a
     "fraction of ceiling" of **1.0264** as proof the shipped quantity was not a proportion.
     So a SINGLE normalisation is not an answer; the estimand is the CURVE.
 (2) R807's g=0 control caught what happens when a denominator is estimated from the same data as
     the numerator. Measured here: **corr(A2, att) = +0.6138 when both come from the same
     annotators, +0.3798 when they come from disjoint halves.** About 0.23 of the coupling is
     SHARED ANNOTATOR NOISE, and dividing by such a denominator injects it rather than removing it.
 (3) The denominator is at least well-defined: per-prompt attainable max ranges **[0.426, 0.911]**,
     mean **0.6863** (matching R804's committed CEIL_ATT 0.686265), with **0 prompts below 0.30**.
     Per-prompt tie rate: mean 0.1397, sd 0.0857, range [0.000, 0.632].

ESTIMAND
E1  the arms' ordering under SIX normalisations: raw · ÷att(shared) · ÷att(SPLIT-HALF) · ÷(1−tie) ·
    subtractive (A2 − att) · ÷√att. All six are defensible and all six are reported.
E2  ⭐ each normalised ordering's rank correlation against the raw ordering, and whether any of the
    arc's committed margins flips sign.
E3  ⭐ the SHARED-NOISE PRICE: the difference between the shared-annotator and split-half versions
    of the same normalisation — the quantity R793 never measured when it swept three.
E4  which normalisation, if any, changes a definitional verdict.

IDENTIFICATION
Only the SPLIT-HALF normalisation is identified: numerator and denominator come from disjoint
annotator halves. The shared version is reported as the CONFOUNDED contrast, never as an answer.
⚠ Splitting halves the annotators behind both quantities, so the split-half cells are noisier; the
half-split sd is reported beside every one of them.

DERIVED BEFORE MEASURING
D1 [DERIVATION] dividing every arm by the SAME per-prompt constant cannot change the ordering on a
   single prompt, but CAN change it after averaging across prompts, because the weights change.
   That is the entire mechanism under test, and stating it first is what stops a null being read as
   "normalisation does nothing".
D2 [DERIVATION] normalising by a constant (not per-prompt) leaves the pooled ordering EXACTLY
   unchanged. PLACEBO.
D3 R793's 1.0264 arose from dividing by CEIL_H = 0.5519, below the arms' own scores. Per-prompt att
   has mean 0.6863, above every arm, so ratios should stay under 1 — and if any exceeds 1 the
   denominator is not a ceiling and the round says so.
D4 an arm whose A2 is proportional to att must normalise FLAT and must move in rank. POSITIVE.

WORLDS
A  ORDERING-ROBUST: every normalisation preserves the ordering (Spearman ≥ 0.9 vs raw) and no
   committed margin flips -> the tie handicap does not drive the arc's conclusions.
B  NORMALISATION-DEPENDENT: at least one defensible normalisation reorders or flips a margin ->
   the arc's numbers are scoped to the raw scale, exactly as R793 warned.
C  NOT IDENTIFIED: the shared and split-half versions of the same normalisation disagree on the
   ordering -> the normalisation cannot be estimated here and the round says so.
CHECKED FIRST: B, because it rescopes every committed number.

PREDICTION MATRIX
                              A                B                 C
Spearman vs raw            >= 0.9           < 0.9 somewhere    any
committed margin flips     none             >= 1               n/a
shared vs split-half       agree            agree              disagree
